5 Crypto Scams That Target Beginners (and How to Spot Them) | Crypto Idiot

6 min read · Crypto Idiot Guides

Here's the uncomfortable truth: scammers don't target crypto experts, because experts don't fall for it. They target people who just arrived. Which means being new makes you the product — unless you learn the playbook. It's a short playbook. Learn these five and you'll dodge 95% of what's out there.

The universal rule: anyone promising guaranteed profits, "doubling" your crypto, or urgent secret opportunities is scamming you. No exceptions, no matter how real it looks.

1. The giveaway scam ("send 1, get 2 back")

You see a YouTube livestream of Elon Musk (or a fake "Binance CEO") saying: send Bitcoin to this address and we'll send back double. The stream has thousands of viewers, professional graphics, a verified-looking checkmark.

Every single one is fake. The "viewers" are bots, the video is recycled or deepfaked, and the "double" never comes. Law enforcement has seized billions from operations running exactly this.

Spot it in seconds: the phrase "send X, receive 2X back." Legitimate people and companies never ask you to send crypto to receive crypto.

2. The romance / trust scam ("pig butchering")

Someone befriends or romances you online over weeks. Eventually they mention their "investment portfolio" and offer to teach you. They walk you through depositing into a slick-looking platform where your balance grows beautifully. When you try to withdraw, there's a "tax" or "unlock fee" — and then the person, the platform, and the money vanish.

Spot it in seconds: anyone you met online who guides you to invest on a specific platform — especially one you found via their link — is running this. Real friends don't route your money through their brokerage.

3. The "guaranteed yield" platform

A website or app promises steady returns: 1% per day, 20% per month, "AI trading bots." Early users can often withdraw small amounts (that's the hook — paid from new deposits). Then the platform collapses, taking everything. This structure is called a Ponzi scheme, and crypto has had several worth tens of billions of dollars.

Spot it in seconds: fixed, high, "guaranteed" returns. Real returns are uncertain and boring. If 1% daily were possible, the owners would quietly become the richest people alive — they wouldn't need your $500.

4. Phishing: fake wallets, fake support, fake emails

You search for "MetaMask download" and click a sponsored ad — it's a clone that steals your seed phrase. Or a "support agent" DMs you about a "suspicious login" and asks you to "verify your wallet." Or an email that looks exactly like Coinbase links to a login page that isn't.

Spot it in seconds: anyone asking for your seed phrase or private key — ever, for any reason — is a thief. Type wallet URLs yourself instead of clicking ads, and bookmark the real ones.

5. The pump-and-dump

A Telegram or Discord group tells you to buy an obscure coin right now before a "huge announcement." The organizers bought it cheap days earlier; your buying pushes the price up, they sell everything at the top, and the price craters seconds later with you holding the bag.

Spot it in seconds: urgency plus a specific coin plus "don't miss this." If a stranger knew about a guaranteed winner, the last thing they'd do is tell 50,000 people about it.

Your 10-second scam filter

  1. Guaranteed or doubled returns? → Scam.
  2. Someone you met online helping you invest? → Scam.
  3. Asked for your seed phrase? → Scam.
  4. Urgency, secrecy, "limited spots"? → Scam.
  5. If it were real, why would they need you? → Scam.

The one-paragraph version

Crypto scams prey on newness: fake giveaways, online friends who "teach" you to invest, guaranteed-return platforms, phishing sites that steal your seed phrase, and pump-and-dump groups. The filter is always the same — guaranteed profit, urgency, or anyone asking for your keys means walk away. Boring skepticism is worth more than any trading strategy.
