"Coin," "token," "crypto" — most people use the words interchangeably, and the crypto world rarely corrects them. But there's a real distinction, and it takes two minutes to learn. **A coin runs on its own blockchain. A token runs on someone else's.** That single difference explains how most of the crypto universe is built.

## What a coin is

A **coin** is native to its own blockchain — it's the fuel the network runs on:

- **Bitcoin (BTC)** on the Bitcoin blockchain
- **Ether (ETH)** on the Ethereum blockchain
- **SOL** on Solana, **ADA** on Cardano, and so on

Coins do the fundamental work of their networks: paying [transaction fees](what-is-gas-fee.html), rewarding the people who secure the chain ([miners](what-is-proof-of-work.html) or validators), and functioning as the network's money. Creating a coin means creating (or forking) an entire blockchain — real infrastructure, real work.

## What a token is

A **token** is an asset built *on top of* an existing blockchain, using that chain's plumbing:

- The vast majority of tokens are [ERC-20 tokens](what-is-defi.html) on Ethereum — a standardized format anyone can use to create a token with a few dozen lines of code.
- Stablecoins like [USDC](what-is-a-stablecoin.html) are tokens. Governance tokens for DeFi protocols are tokens. [NFTs](what-is-an-nft.html) are a special, non-divisible kind of token.

Tokens inherit the host chain's security and infrastructure for free: they don't need their own miners, their own validators, or their own wallets. That low barrier to entry is why there are millions of tokens and only a few dozen significant coins — and it's exactly why the token world contains both genuine innovation and industrial-scale junk.

## The quick intuition

- **A coin is like a country's currency** — backed by (and inseparable from) its own national infrastructure.
- **A token is like a gift card** — a claim issued *by someone*, running on someone else's rails. The gift card's value depends entirely on the issuer, not the payment network it rides on.

A stablecoin is a gift card issued by a company. A DeFi governance token is a gift card for voting rights in a protocol. A meme coin is, at best, a gift card for a joke — and at worst, a gift card for nothing (see [rug pulls](what-is-a-rug-pull.html) and [meme coins](what-is-a-meme-coin.html)).

## Why the distinction matters to you

**1. Evaluating projects.** "We're launching our own blockchain" is a much bigger claim than "we're launching a token." The first promises infrastructure; the second is an afternoon of coding. When someone pitches you a project, the coin/token question tells you how much substance to expect.

**2. Understanding supply games.** Tokens are trivially easy to mint, and their creators control the faucet — which is why supply tricks, insider allocations, and unlock schedules dominate token projects. Coins with a decade of history and transparent issuance (like Bitcoin's [halving schedule](what-is-bitcoin-halving.html)) face a completely different trust calculus.

**3. Moving assets safely.** Tokens live at *addresses* on their host chain, and sending them means paying fees in the host coin (ETH for Ethereum tokens, SOL for Solana tokens). This trips up beginners constantly: you need a small amount of the host coin in your wallet to move any token at all.

**4. Filtering hype.** Ninety-plus percent of "the next Bitcoin" pitches are tokens launched in a day riding Ethereum's credibility. Knowing the difference won't make you rich, but it will quietly delete most bad pitches before they cost you money.

<div class="callout"><b>One-line version:</b> if the asset has its own blockchain, it's a coin. If it borrows someone else's, it's a token. Everything else — the use case, the team, the promises — is judged separately from that fact.</div>

## The one-paragraph version

A coin is native to its own blockchain — Bitcoin on Bitcoin, Ether on Ethereum — and serves as that network's fuel, paying fees and rewarding the people who secure it, while a token is an asset built on top of someone else's chain, inheriting the host's security and infrastructure for free. Tokens are easy to create, which is why millions exist, from legitimate stablecoins and governance tokens to a long tail of junk where supply tricks and insider allocations are standard; coins are hard to create, which is why only a few dozen matter. For a beginner, the distinction is a filter: "own blockchain" signals infrastructure ambition, "token on Ethereum" signals an afternoon of coding, and knowing which you're looking at quietly deletes most bad investment pitches before they reach your wallet.
