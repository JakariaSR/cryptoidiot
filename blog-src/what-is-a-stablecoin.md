Bitcoin can drop 10% while you drink a coffee. For traders, that's normal. For anyone actually *using* crypto to send, save, or park money between trades, it's a dealbreaker. Enter the stablecoin: a crypto token designed to be boring. **The whole point of a stablecoin is that one coin stays worth roughly one US dollar — today, tomorrow, and during the chaos.**

## What a stablecoin actually is

A stablecoin is a token on a blockchain whose value is *pegged* to something stable, almost always the US dollar. One USDT or USDC is designed to always be redeemable for $1.

You get the best of both worlds: the price stability of dollars, and the crypto rails — you can send any amount, anywhere, in minutes, at any hour, with fees measured in cents.

- **For trading:** instead of selling Bitcoin back to dollars (slow, with fees), traders swap to a stablecoin instantly and wait for the next opportunity.
- **For sending money:** a worker in one country can receive stablecoins from abroad without a bank wire's fees and 3-day wait.
- **For savings (in unstable economies):** people in countries with high inflation sometimes hold stablecoins to protect purchasing power. It works — until it doesn't (see risks below).

## How do they keep the price at $1?

There are three designs, and understanding the difference matters more than any specific coin's name.

**1. Fiat-backed (the big ones: USDT, USDC).** The company behind the coin holds real dollars and short-term government bonds in reserve, roughly $1 for every token. You trust that they actually hold the reserves and will honor redemptions. These dominate the market — USDT and USDC together account for the overwhelming majority of stablecoin trading volume.

**2. Crypto-backed (DAI and similar).** Instead of dollars in a bank, the coin is backed by other crypto locked in a smart contract, usually over-collateralized (you lock $150+ of ETH to mint $100 of stablecoin). More transparent, more complex.

**3. Algorithmic (the danger zone).** Coins that keep their peg purely through software incentives — "the code will balance it" — with no real reserves behind them. In May 2022, the biggest one, TerraUSD, lost its peg and went from $1 to effectively zero in about a week, wiping out tens of billions of dollars. The lesson stuck: **if nothing real backs the peg, the peg is a promise, not a mechanism.**

<div class="callout"><b>The 5-second test:</b> before trusting any stablecoin, ask one question — "what exactly backs this, and can I verify it?" Fiat-backed means trusting audits and regulators. Crypto-backed means trusting code and collateral. Algorithmic means trusting hope. Choose accordingly.</div>

## What stablecoins are actually used for

Strip away the trading culture and stablecoins solve real problems:

1. **A parking spot between trades.** Volatility is the tax on crypto activity; stablecoins let you sit still without leaving the ecosystem.
2. **Cheap global transfers.** Sending $200 across borders via banks can cost $20–50 and take days. A stablecoin transfer costs under a dollar and settles in minutes.
3. **A dollar substitute in inflation-hit countries.** In places where local currency loses value fast, a phone-based dollar is genuinely useful financial infrastructure.
4. **The base pair of all crypto trading.** Almost every "altcoin" on every exchange is priced against stablecoins, not dollars. That's why stablecoin supply is often read as a barometer of money waiting on the sidelines.

## The 3 risks nobody puts in the ads

**1. Depegging.** A stablecoin's whole value is the peg — so when one wobbles, panic follows. USDC famously wobbled in 2023 (it recovered within days) when markets learned part of its reserves sat in a troubled bank. Even the "safe" ones can briefly trade at 97 or 98 cents during stress. If you'd panic at a 3% dip, stablecoins aren't risk-free for you.

**2. Issuer risk.** Fiat-backed stablecoins are IOUs. If the issuer is mismanaged, censored by regulators, or — worst case — fraudulent, the tokens can become worthless. This is why transparency matters: the trustworthy issuers publish regular attestations of their reserves.

**3. Regulatory risk.** Governments are still deciding how to treat stablecoins. Rules can change which coins exchanges offer, what you must do to use them, and what happens to the ones that don't comply. For a beginner: stick to the large, transparent, heavily traded stablecoins and you'll face the least surprises.

## Which stablecoins do beginners actually use?

In practice, the market sorts itself simply:

- **USDT (Tether)** — the biggest by far, deepest liquidity everywhere, but a longer history of questions about reserve transparency.
- **USDC (from Circle)** — smaller, but positioned as the most transparent and regulated of the big two, with regular public attestations.
- **DAI** — the main decentralized option, backed by crypto collateral instead of company promises.

For a beginner, the practical answer is boring: use whichever major stablecoin your exchange supports, in small amounts, understanding it's a tool for moving between trades — not a magic savings account.

## The one-paragraph version

A stablecoin is a crypto token engineered to stay worth $1, so people can move money on blockchains without Bitcoin's rollercoaster. The big ones (USDT, USDC) do it by holding real-dollar reserves; some do it with crypto collateral; the failed ones tried to do it with math and hope — and collapsed when trust broke. Stablecoins are genuinely useful for trading, cheap international transfers, and dodging inflation, but they're not risk-free: they can briefly lose their peg, they depend on the issuer's honesty, and regulators are still rewriting the rulebook. Use the big, transparent ones for what they're good at — a parking spot, not a vault.
