It's the question every newcomer eventually asks, usually at 1 AM after reading a scary headline: **can Bitcoin be hacked?** The honest answer has two parts, and keeping them straight protects you better than either answer alone: **the Bitcoin network itself has never been hacked in its entire history — but almost everything around Bitcoin gets hacked constantly.** Understanding the difference between those two sentences is most of crypto security.

## Part 1: The network — undefeated since 2009

Bitcoin's core ledger — the [blockchain](what-is-a-blockchain.html) recording who owns what — has run continuously since January 2009 without being successfully attacked. Through booms, crashes, nation-state hostility, and a decade and a half of the world's best hackers knowing exactly where to aim, no one has ever:

- Rewrote Bitcoin's history to steal coins from the ledger
- Spent the same coins twice (the "double spend" the whole design prevents)
- Counterfeited bitcoins or inflated the supply beyond the coded schedule

The reason is the same one covered in [proof of work](what-is-proof-of-work.html): attacking Bitcoin means out-computing the combined honest network — a hardware and energy bill measured in billions, sustained in secret, with failure costing everything spent. The network's security grows with every miner competing on it, and after fifteen years that mountain is genuinely enormous.

Honest caveat: "never hacked" is about the *record*, not predictions. Brilliant people disagree about theoretical long-term threats (quantum computing gets the most press; most serious researchers consider it a solvable future problem rather than a present one, since the ecosystem can upgrade its cryptography). But as a statement about the world you live in today, the ledger's track record is simply a fact.

## Part 2: Everything around the network — where the bodies are

If the network is a fortress, the ecosystem around it is a busy market square — and that's where essentially all the theft happens:

**Exchanges.** The single biggest source of losses in Bitcoin's history. Mt. Gox lost hundreds of thousands of BTC. FTX collapsed with customer funds. Dozens of smaller exchanges have been breached or vanished. The pattern is always the same: coins held by a company, in a form the company controlled. This is precisely why "not your keys, not your coins" exists, and why the [cold storage](what-is-cold-storage.html) ladder matters.

**Individual wallets and users.** Malware that swaps clipboard addresses. Phishing sites that steal [seed phrases](what-is-a-seed-phrase.html). Fake apps in app stores. SIM-swap attacks on exchange accounts. Brute-forced weak passwords. These attacks don't hack Bitcoin — they hack *you*, which is vastly easier than attacking the math.

**Scams.** Fake giveaways, romance fraud, [rug pulls](what-is-a-rug-pull.html), "guaranteed yield" Ponzi schemes. Billions leave the ecosystem this way annually, voluntarily handed over by hopeful people. No hacking required — just persuasion.

The ledger records every one of these thefts faithfully. Bitcoin's security was never the problem; the humans holding the keys were.

The entire security industry of crypto exists because <b>you</b> — not the protocol — are the practical attack surface. ## "But I heard about the 51% attack…"

You'll encounter this term, so here's the honest version. A 51% attack means controlling most of the network's mining power, which in theory could let an attacker reverse recent transactions or censor others. Two things to know:

1. **On Bitcoin, it's practically impossible today** — the required computing power is beyond any realistic attacker, and the cost would exceed any conceivable gain. Smaller coins with thin mining networks *have* suffered such attacks, which is another reason major networks dominate.
2. **Even a successful attack couldn't steal old coins or rewrite deep history** — it could only mess with very recent transactions. The deeper the confirmations, the more set in stone the record becomes.

So: real concept, real threat to tiny obscure chains, theoretical footnote for Bitcoin.

## The practical takeaway

So — can Bitcoin be hacked? **The network: no successful attack ever recorded, by design.** **Your holdings: absolutely, if you're careless** — and careless usually means someone else holding your keys (exchange), you handing over your keys (phishing), or you breaking key-handling rules (no backup, shared phrase, weak security).

Which means your security posture is entirely in your hands, and it's refreshingly simple: follow the [checklist](crypto-security-checklist.html), hold your own keys properly, and treat every promise of easy money as the attack it probably is. The fortress will keep standing either way.

## The one-paragraph version

Bitcoin's core network has never been hacked since 2009 — no one has ever rewritten its ledger, counterfeited coins, or double-spent, because attacking it would require out-computing billions of dollars of honest mining power, which is the design working as intended. But nearly everything around the network — exchanges, wallets, and especially users — gets compromised constantly, and that's where essentially all real-world losses come from: stolen seed phrases, breached exchanges, phishing, and scams that need no hacking at all, just persuasion. The famous "51% attack" is a real threat to tiny coins but practically impossible against Bitcoin today. So the honest answer is: the fortress has never fallen, and your tent outside it is only as safe as your own habits — which is why self-custody done right, security basics, and skepticism of easy money are the whole game.
