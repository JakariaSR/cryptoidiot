You've bought some crypto, and now the internet wants you to choose between a "hot wallet" and a "cold wallet" — as if you asked where to keep your money and got two options: your pocket, or a vault in another country. Here's the thing: **both are right, for different amounts of money.** The whole trick is knowing which one protects what.

## What a hot wallet actually is

A hot wallet is any crypto wallet that is **connected to the internet**. That includes:

- Exchange accounts (Coinbase, Kraken, and friends holding your coins)
- Mobile wallet apps (MetaMask, Trust Wallet)
- Browser extension wallets
- Desktop wallets on an internet-connected computer

The upside: instant access. You can trade, send, and connect to apps in seconds. The downside: **anything connected to the internet can potentially be reached by someone else** — through phishing, malware, a hacked exchange, or a convincing fake website. Convenience and attack surface travel together.

## What a cold wallet actually is

A cold wallet is a wallet whose [private key](what-is-a-private-key.html) **never touches the internet**. The key is generated and stored on an offline device — typically a small hardware device that looks like a USB stick, or in careful cases, a properly made paper backup kept somewhere physically safe.

Because the key never goes online, remote attackers can't reach it. No phishing link can trick it, no malware can read it, no exchange collapse can take it. The tradeoff: moving money takes a few extra steps, and **you are your own bank in the most literal sense** — lose the device and the backup, and the coins are gone forever.

## The standard split: think checking vs savings

Experienced users almost all land on the same arrangement, and it's refreshingly un-exotic:

- **Hot wallet = checking account.** Enough for what you're actively using — trading, learning, small amounts. Money you'd be upset to lose, but not ruined.
- **Cold wallet = savings account.** The bulk of your holdings, touched rarely, protected offline. This is the core logic behind the whole [cold storage](what-is-cold-storage.html) approach.

There is no official percentage — the right split depends on your amounts, your patience, and how much of your money you actively use. A person with $150 of crypto has no reason to buy a hardware device; a person with $15,000 sleeping on an exchange has a reason to rethink.

## Common hot wallet mistakes

1. **Treating an exchange like a savings account.** The exchange holds the keys, not you. "Not your keys, not your coins" exists because of everything in [can Bitcoin be hacked](can-bitcoin-be-hacked.html) — the network survives every exchange collapse; the users don't always.
2. **Keeping more in a browser wallet than you'd carry in cash.** Browser extension wallets are the most phished target in crypto. Small amounts only.
3. **Ignoring the basics.** A hot wallet with a [good seed phrase backup](what-is-a-seed-phrase.html) beats a cold wallet with a sloppy one.

## So which one do YOU need?

Honestly: **both, in proportion.** A hot wallet (or a reputable exchange, for small amounts) for the money you're actively moving, and proper cold storage for anything you're holding long-term. The only people who need to choose one are choosing between losing-to-hackers and losing-to-themselves — and that decision gets made by looking at your own habits, not by ideology.

## The one-paragraph version

A hot wallet is connected to the internet (exchange accounts, mobile and browser wallets) and is convenient but attackable; a cold wallet keeps your private keys permanently offline and is far safer but slower and fully your own responsibility. Almost everyone ends up using both — a hot wallet like a checking account for active money, and cold storage like a savings account for the bulk of holdings — with the split depending on how much you hold and how careful you are. The classic mistakes are keeping serious savings on an exchange and keeping active spending money in a heavily phished browser wallet.
