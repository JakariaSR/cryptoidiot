In 2021, a collage of digital images sold for $69 million. The art world lost its mind, the internet minted a million memes, and a new three-letter acronym entered the mainstream: NFT. Strip away the madness and the concept is clean: **an NFT is a blockchain record proving that one specific wallet owns one specific digital item — and that the item is unique, not interchangeable.**

## Breaking down the name

- **Token** — a digital entry on a blockchain, like the coins you already know.
- **Non-fungible** — the opposite of money. One dollar is identical to any other dollar; they're *fungible*. A painting, a house, or a concert ticket is *not* interchangeable with any other — each is one of a kind. Non-fungible = unique.

So an NFT is essentially a **certificate of authenticity that can't be forged or duplicated**, because it lives on a blockchain. Anyone can copy a JPEG — but only one wallet can hold the token that says *this* is the original, officially linked item.

That distinction mattered because before NFTs, there was no way to prove you "owned" a digital file at all. Files copy infinitely. NFTs gave digital objects the one property they always lacked: provable uniqueness and provenance.

## What NFTs are actually for

The 2021 headlines were about art, but the underlying tool is broader:

- **Digital art and collectibles** — the famous use case. Artists can sell directly, program royalties into future resales, and prove authenticity.
- **Gaming items** — swords, skins, characters that you truly own and can trade outside the game. (The promise is bigger than current delivery, but the idea is real.)
- **Memberships and tickets** — an NFT as a concert pass or community membership that can't be counterfeited and can be verified instantly.
- **Domain names** — blockchain-based web addresses (like `yourname.eth`) that you own outright, no yearly registrar.

Notice what these share: proof of uniqueness or ownership for something digital, where counterfeiting used to be free.

## "But I can just right-click and save the image"

Yes — and that's fine. This is the most misunderstood part of NFTs, so let's be precise about what you own:

- **You own the token** — the blockchain entry pointing to the item, with your wallet as its owner. It's verifiable, portable, and transferable.
- **You usually don't own the copyright.** The artist typically keeps it, same as when you buy a print of a painting. Owning the NFT doesn't grant you the right to print merch or license the image.
- **Anyone can view/copy the file.** The Mona Lisa's image is on a billion postcards; the museum still owns the original. Scarcity of *the original* is what the token certifies, not scarcity of the pixels.

Whether that distinction has value is genuinely debatable — and reasonable people land on both sides. What matters is you understand what you're buying before you buy it.

## Why prices went insane (and then didn't)

The 2021 mania followed a textbook bubble pattern:

1. A new tool appears with a real use case (art royalties, provable scarcity).
2. Early sales make headlines; FOMO floods in.
3. Speculation detaches from utility — people buy whatever's minting, art quality irrelevant.
4. Celebrity cash-grabs and copy-paste projects flood supply.
5. The frenzy exhausts itself; volume and prices collapse 90%+ from the peak.

Many early collections are now worth a fraction of their peak, and most 2021 projects are functionally dead. The survivors tend to share traits with any durable asset: real community, real utility, and creators who kept building after the hype left. The technology outlived the mania — which is usually how new tools work.

## The 3 risks before you buy anything

**1. Scams are everywhere in this niche.** Fake collections impersonating famous ones, phishing sites that steal your wallet when you "mint," and Discord DMs promising whitelist spots. Rule: never click a mint link you didn't find from the project's own verified channel.

**2. Liquidity is thin.** You can buy an NFT in seconds; selling one can take weeks or never happen at all. An NFT's "floor price" is an illusion of a market — there's no guarantee a buyer exists at that number.

**3. Fees and bridges multiply costs.** Minting and trading on Ethereum involves gas fees (sometimes tens of dollars), and moving NFTs between chains adds more. Small purchases can be eaten by transaction costs.

<div class="callout"><b>The honest beginner position:</b> treat NFTs like collectible art, not like crypto. You buy art because you love it and can afford to lose the money — not because a line on a chart promises to go up. If a project needs you to recruit friends or promises returns, it's not art; it's a scheme wearing art's clothes.</div>

## The one-paragraph version

An NFT is a blockchain token that certifies one wallet as the owner of one unique digital item — solving the old problem that digital files could be copied infinitely but never provably owned. You own the certificate, not usually the copyright, and anyone can still copy the image itself; what you hold is verifiable originality, like owning a signed print while posters flood the market. NFTs have genuine uses in art royalties, gaming items, memberships, and domains, but the 2021 boom was mostly speculation, and most of those prices collapsed — so buy NFTs the way you'd buy art, because you value the item, never as an investment you can't afford to lose, and stay far away from anything promising returns or recruiting you.
