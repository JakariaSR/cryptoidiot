Every crypto bull market produces the same headline: some coin you never heard of, named after a dog or a frog or a misspelled word, is up 5,000% and minting millionaires. These are **meme coins: cryptocurrencies whose value comes from internet culture, community hype, and speculation — not from any product, revenue, or technological innovation.** Dogecoin (the original, started as a literal joke in 2013) was the first. It will not be the last. Understanding how they work is mostly about understanding how they hurt people.

## What a meme coin actually is

Technically, a meme coin is just a token — software on a [blockchain](what-is-a-blockchain.html), like any other. What distinguishes it is everything else:

- **No business behind it.** No product, no revenue, no roadmap that anyone expects to deliver. The "use case" is the joke and the community.
- **Huge or unlimited supply.** Meme coins typically launch with billions or trillions of tokens, which is why their prices read like "$0.000008" — and why beginners mistake them for "cheap" (revisit [market cap vs price](market-cap-vs-price.html) before believing that).
- **Pure attention economy.** The price is driven by social media momentum: celebrities, influencers, community raids, trending hashtags. When the attention moves on, nothing fundamental is left holding the price up.

Dogecoin is the exception that proves the rule: over a decade old, with one of crypto's most enduring communities, it has survived multiple cycles almost by accident. Thousands of copycats — Shiba Inu, Pepe, and endless daily newcomers — have not.

## Why they explode (and why they collapse)

The lifecycle is so consistent you can set a watch by it:

1. **A meme is born.** Someone launches a token riffing on a joke, a celebrity, or a current event. Costs minutes; costs nothing.
2. **The crowd arrives.** A viral tweet, an influencer mention, a community push — early buyers pour in, price rockets, screenshots of "gains" flood social media.
3. **The feeding frenzy.** FOMO does the rest. New buyers arrive purely because the number went up. [Market cap](market-cap-vs-price.html) briefly swells into the billions.
4. **The exit.** Early insiders — often the token's own creators — sell into the frenzy. The attention peaks, the momentum dies, and the price collapses 80–99%. Late buyers hold the losses.

This is a textbook [pump-and-dump](common-crypto-scams.html) engine, even when nobody technically "runs" it: the structure guarantees that the people who arrive last feed the people who arrived first. Attention, unlike a product, always moves on.

<div class="callout"><b>The 30-second self-check:</b> ask "what makes this coin worth anything if the meme stops being funny?" If the honest answer is "nothing," then you're not investing — you're buying a ticket in a lottery where the house got in first. That's not automatically forbidden; it just must be priced like the gamble it is.</div>

## Are meme coins always scams?

Not always — and the distinction matters. Dogecoin, for all its absurdity, is transparent about being a joke, with no founder stash promising riches. That's honest gambling.

The genuinely dangerous ones check the [rug pull](what-is-a-rug-pull.html) boxes: anonymous creators holding huge hidden supply, marketing that promises utility "coming soon," paid influencers denying they're paid. A coin can be a meme and a scam simultaneously — the meme is the lure. The 5 warning signs from the rug pull guide apply with double force here, because meme coins move exactly the speed scams need.

## The 4 rules if you can't resist

You don't need our permission, and we'll be honest: some people have made real money on meme coins. The survivors share a pattern:

1. **Only money you can fully afford to lose.** Not "lose some" — lose *all*, because all is a common outcome. Money for rent, debt, or savings is never meme-coin money.
2. **Size it like a lottery ticket.** Meme coins should be a tiny, deliberate sliver of a portfolio — for most people, under 1–2%. If a meme coin position would ruin your week if it went to zero, it's too big.
3. **Never chase a pump.** Buying after a 500% week means buying from insiders who are selling. If you must, buying the boring dips of established names beats chasing vertical green candles of new ones.
4. **Decide your exit before you buy.** "If it doubles, I sell half." Write it down. Meme coins trigger the exact [greed and panic](bull-vs-bear-markets.html) instincts that make people buy tops and hold through collapses.

## The one-paragraph version

A meme coin is a crypto token whose entire value comes from internet culture and community hype rather than any product or revenue — technically just software, economically pure attention. They explode because social momentum and FOMO compound, and they collapse because attention always moves on, leaving late buyers holding the losses in a structure that works like a pump-and-dump even when no one runs it. They're not all outright scams — Dogecoin's honest joke has survived over a decade — but the meme is the perfect lure for the [rug pulls](what-is-a-rug-pull.html) that dominate the space. If you choose to play anyway: size it like a lottery ticket you can fully afford to lose, never chase a vertical pump, and write your exit rule down before you buy — the crowd never cheers when it's time to leave.
